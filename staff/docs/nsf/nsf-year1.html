ANNUAL REPORT YEAR ONE (March-December 2001)
PIE NETWORK NSF GRANT 0087813

1. Research and Educational Activities
The first year of the PIE Network project focused on staff development and on creating new PIE workshops and other public activities and events at each of the six museums.

FORT WORTH MINDFEST
In August 2001, project staff from MIT and all of the PIE Network museums gathered at the Fort Worth Museum of Science and History to contribute to running workshops and other activities at the first public MindFest (http://www.smm.org/pie/FW/). This new form of public event -- billed as "a festival for hobbyists, inventors, tinkerers, educators, children, researchers, artists, engineers, parents, and everyone with a curious or playful mind" - offered local families and other community members a full day of activities with a focus on combining science, invention, exploration, and technology with art, dance, creativity, and play. Organization of the event was led by Fort Worth PIE staff members Chip Lindsey and Julie Cross-Steele.

Audience
The Fort Worth Museum MindFest was designated a free day to encourage participation by families and individuals in the greater Dallas-Fort Worth metro area who would not otherwise have been able to afford to come. The Fort Worth MindFest was promoted on local television and radio, as well as through flyers at local community centers. Fort Worth Museum staff also promoted the event to Big Brothers/Big Sisters organizations, local Girl Scouts, and the local Boys and Girls Clubs. The event was also promoted with teachers in local schools. Forty teachers signed in to receive continuing education credit on the day of the event, and many of these teachers participated in the roundtable discussions, held in Fort Worth's Center of Adult Learning.

The MindFest event drew a total of approximately 4,700 visitors. The event included a range of experiences for participants to become involved in and reflect on playful invention and learning -- including a �construction zone�, roundtables, playshops, and other special presentations and activities, described below.

Construction Zone
For the MindFest event, a large area in the museum's ExploraZone exhibit area was transformed into a Construction Zone, a space where families worked on creating their own inventive creations using a mixture of construction materials, programmable bricks, and art supplies. Staff from across the PIE Network assisted visitors ages 3 and up with building, creating, and learning to do simple programming with MIT�s programmable �cricket� technology (see http://cricket.media.mit.edu/). The area was extremely popular the entire day (from 10am when it opened to closing at 7pm), though staff found it logistically challenging to keep materials stocked and organized while accommodating thousands of people over the course of the day.

Roundtables
Instead of offering panel presentations or lectures, Fort Worth decided to offer a series of informal discussion roundtables, designed to encourage participation by parents, teachers, and other interested visitors in issues related to innovative applications of technology in education. Discussions were moderated by professors, educators, artists, and others from across the PIE Network, and included the following topics:

  • Toys, Play, and �Middle Tech�;
  • Playful Invention;
  • Rethinking the Classroom
  • The Arts and Technology;

Descriptions of the Roundtables are posted at http://www.smm.org/pie/FW/roundtable.html

Playshops
As part of the MindFest, Fort Worth scheduled eleven PIE Workshops (or "playshops") that involved children and adults in artistic invention. Some workshops were for young children, others were for mixed ages, from twelve years through adult. Workshop leaders included staff from across the PIE Network as well as local educators. The playshops reflected a range of use of traditional art materials and digital technology. A total of 220 people participated in the playshops, with almost all the playshops filling prior to the day of the event.

In some workshops, participants made innovative use of traditional craft materials � for example, making sculptures out of matchsticks (led by Beth Secor of the American Visionary Art Museum) or creating automata, using a plastic cup and wooden dowels and spheres (led by PIE advisors Sue Jackson and Sarah of the Cabaret Mechanical Theatre). Many of the playshops made use of the Media Lab�s �cricket� technology.For example: Make Your Own Wacky Water Fountains (developed by Diane Willow and Casey Smith of the MIT Media Lab), Animatronic Puppet Theater (developed by Bakhtiar Mikhak of the MIT Media Lab), and Invent An Amazing Spin Art  Machine (by Keith Braafladt and Margaret Pezalla-Granlund of the Science Museum of Minnesota).

Other Special Activities
Over the course of the day, a number of other special activities and presentations were offered for visitors, led by a combination of local presenters and staff from across the PIE Network. For example, Diane Willow of the MIT Media Lab worked with Fort Worth Museum staff to help young visitors design and make Mylar balloons to become part of a floating sculpture in the Museum Rotunda. In the Museum's Hands-On Science space, visitors got the chance to explore the inner workings of washing machines and refrigerators by taking them apart, then to make inventions of the own using the gears and other parts. In an unusual and well-received presentation entitled, "Crickets, Cha Cha and Swing Swing," a local dance group performed a dance with little cricket-controlled robots responding to human dancers movements.

Plans
The Fort Worth MindFest, as the first public MindFest, served as a special learning opportunity for staff from all of the PIE Museums (see Section 3, on Training and Staff Development for more information). Fort Worth is currently planning its second MindFest event, tentatively scheduled for fall of 2002.


LEMELSON CENTER
In October, a group of 14 PIE staff (from Exploratorium, MIT Media Lab, Science Museum of Minnesota, and the Lemelson Center) worked with exhibit developers during an intensive two-day working design meeting held in the Science Museum of Minnesota's Learning Technologies Center. The focus of the charette was to explore cricket-related connections to exhibit components for the Lemelson Center's �Invention at Play� exhibit (http://www.astc.org/exhibitions/invention/dinvention.htm). Photos and descriptions of this initial exhibit charette are posted at: http://heisenberg.exploratorium.edu/scrapbook/charette/charette.html

Participants in the charette went back to their museums to work on further prototypes. A follow-up charette is scheduled to be held at the Exploratorium in early March 2002, with finalized prototypes due to be reviewed and selected for the exhibition by the Lemelson Center staff at MIT at the end of March, 2002.

Lemelson PIE staff members are also involved in intensive planning for the public MindFest event to be held August 16, 17 and 18, 2002 at the National Museum of American History, following the opening of the Invention at Play exhibition.


MIT MUSEUM
Every November, the MIT Museum holds its annual Friday After Thanksgiving (F.A.T.) Chain Reaction, an event that encourages visitors to make their own contraptions, link them with those of other people, and set them off in one spectacular chain reaction. This year, as part of the PIE Network, MIT Museum staff offered Chain Reaction Studio, two three-hour public programs in preparation for the F.A.T. Chain Reaction event. These workshops provided the opportunity for 30 families to learn to create a contraption that integrated programmable technology.

MIT Museum staff also launched an ongoing series of monthly workshops for families (offered the first and third Saturday of each month) called "MIT Invention Studio." The workshops involve participants in designing and constructing contraptions using a variety of building materials, including motors, sensors, and crickets. MIT Museum PIE staff member Stephanie Hunt worked with Diane Willow and MIT graduate students to develop these new PIE workshops, with a different theme each month.

The special theme for MIT Invention Studio during the month of November, 2001, was sensors. One of the major goals during this session was to make the connection between the use of sensors in the museum's Artificial Intelligence exhibit and the application of sensors in the creations of participants. The special theme for the month of December, 2001, was sound. One of the major goals during this session was to present the use of new technology as a tool with which to create a wide variety of sounds, and to empower participants to create sounds that could interact with their environment.

Stephanie Hunt of MIT Museum has developed an online scrapbook with documentation and reflection on each of these new workshops to the PIE staff website. This scrapbook is serving as a resource for current PIE staff across the Network and will serve as a basis for the future development of PIE Project Pamphlets.

Future MIT Invention Studio workshops are planned through May 2002, with each workshop incorporating cricket technology to support invention and exploration in new ways and highlighting different themes and concepts in science, art, and engineering.

AMERICAN VISIONARY ART MUSEUM
In November 2001, the American Visionary Art Museum (AVAM) offered its first PIE workshop, as part of its "Exquisite Trash" series of workshops incorporating recycled materials which involved approximately 285 participants. Rahul Bhargava (a graduate student from the MIT Media Lab) and Meg Goetz (a friend of the PIE Network) helped lead the workshop entitled Cricket Crazy. The workshop involved participants in creating their own sculptures, using crickets to animate recycled parts from used toys and other discarded electronic devices.

Beth Secor of AVAM is currently planning a week of Art Car workshops (culminating in a symposium and parade) in July 2002. Karen Wilkinson and Mike Petrich from the Exploratorium will work with an artist and the general public to create an art car employing cricket technology.


EXPLORATORIUM
PIE Staff at the Exploratorium have developed several new PIE-style activities for the general public. One particularly popular activity grew out of an Exploratorium public event in March 2001, focusing on the theme of �ice.� At this event (part of the Exploratorium�s �2nd Wednesday� series featuring interactive works by local artists), PIE staff Karen Wilkinson and Mike Petrich set up a way for visitors to play music by moving their hands and other materials over a blocks of ice (which were connected to a cricket and small MIDI music synthesizer). This "Ice Theremin" engaged approximately 100 visitors in experimenting with the unusual effects that salt, sugar, and other household materials have in changing electrical resistance, with musical sounds corresponding to the amount of resistance.

Karen and Mike then refined this prototype activity and offered it as a special activity during the Fort Worth MindFest in August. Based on the successful experience offering this activity on the exhibition floor in Fort Worth, Karen and Mike further developed the ice theremin for high school and college Explainers at the Exploratorium to learn about and offer as an interactive demonstration for visitors, in conjunction with the Exploratorium's series of webcasts from Antarctica. These interactive demonstrations began in December, 2001, and an interesting exchange developed between questions generated by Explainers and visitors, and potential explanations offered by Senior Scientist Paul Doherty who was in Antarctica at the time.

Exploratorium PIE staff also developed math-related activities using crickets around the theme of Spirograph -- for example, blowing bubbles that mapped to circular patterns on a computer screen. These activities were first prototyped for and tried out by more than 100 families during Family Fun Night, in October 2001.

A web site on each of these activities was developed for sharing these ideas with other museums across the PIE Network.


SCIENCE MUSEUM OF MINNESOTA
Science Museum of Minnesota (SMM) developed a variety of new workshopsinvolving cricket technology. In preparation for the Fort Worth MindFest, museum staffers Keith Braafladt and Margaret Pezalla-Granlund developed a PIE workshop called, "Spin Art." Following the MindFest, Science Museum staff led the Spin Art activity at the afterschool youth center in Landfall, a mobile home community. Boys and girls ages 4to 11 participated, painting with motorized brushes programmed to twirl using LEGO MindStorms RCX bricks.

SMM PIE staff also developed a number of activities making use of the new speech recorder cricket bus device developed by MIT Media Lab. One sound workshop, held at the afterschool center in the Landfall community, first involved young people in experimenting with sound effects, writing a short story, and then using crickets with speech recorders to record sounds for their story (for example, for a story about playing basketball, participants found and recorded the sound of a bouncing ball, a ball going into a basket, and cheering).

Staff have also developed and held a workshop called "Sound Symphony" that involves children (ages 8-13) in using a combination of LEGO MindStorms RCX brick and recycled materials to create and program their own instruments. At the end of the week-long workshop, students work together as a group to create an orchestra from their invented instruments. In addition, PIE staff are currently developing a series of sound activities making use of crickets as part of the Sound Project, a collaboration between the Minnesota Orchestra and SMM working with teachers and students from Minneapolis-St. Paul public schools.

Beginning in October, PIE staff from SMM began working with groups of students on Mondays and Wednesdays, in collaboration with Karen Thimmesch at the Museum Magnet Elementary School in St. Paul. On Mondays, students are writing graphical computer programs that model real-world, physical phenomenon that interests them (such as skateboarding, vehicles, or fish jumping out of water) and programming these models to react to input from sensors. On Wednesdays, seven teams of students are each writing and developing their own puppet show, creating cricket-based animatronic puppets and building and programming cricket-controlled stage sets.


USING AND DEVELOPING MIT CRICKET TECHNOLOGY
The Media Lab and the PIE museums have collaborated on an iterative process for the design of new cricket technologies, with Media Lab researchers continually adapting the technologies based on feedback and suggestions from PIE museum staff members. For example, Media Lab researchers developed a special water-pump device for use in an "Aquaducks" workshop at the MIT Museum -- and later used at the Fort Worth museum. Based on suggestions from PIE museum staff, Media Lab researchers also developed a relay device that makes it possible to control a much wider variety of electrical devices from the cricket.

The Media Lab team has put special focus on the development of new software to make the programming of cricket devices easier and more intuitive. Based on feedback from the museums, Media Lab researchers developed an updated version of the graphical programming language LogoBlocks; in this new version, users can customize the palette of commands based on the activity (for example, adding special programmable blocks for workshops involving water pumps). The MIT team also integrated its text-based programming environments into a single package called Jackal, which can now be used to control both MIT crickets and the commercial LEGO MindStorms brick.

Media Lab researchers created a special collection of cricket technology, called a "Cricket Survival Kit", for each PIE museum staff member who participated in the week-long PIE staff workshop leading up to the MindFest in Fort Worth. After the workshop, the PIE staff members took the kits back to their respective museums so that they could continue to work with (and learn about) the cricket technology.

MIT graduate students developed a web site, called Chirp (http://chirp.media.mit.edu/), to provide a place for discussion of technical and conceptual issues related to the use of MIT cricket technology. PIE staff members have used to site to ask questions and share ideas with one another.

Mitchel Resnick and Diane Willow of the Media Lab each presented at sessions at the Association of Science and Technology Centers conference in October 2001. Following these presentations (organized by the Lemelson Center), staff from three major science-and-technology museums approached the MIT researchers and expressed interest in joining the PIE Network activities.


2. Findings
As stated in the original proposal, we subcontracted with the Education Development Center�s Center for Children and Technology (EDC/CCT) to conduct a three-year formative and summative evaluation.A three-person research team from CCT has been assigned to the PIE Network.The research team and the key representatives in the PIE Network discussed the preliminary evaluation plan and agreed to focus year-one research efforts on the first public MindFest event, held in Fort Worth, and the intensive weeklong staff development workshop (for staff members from all the PIE museums) that preceded the event.

All three of the CCT researchers spent some time at the August 2001 workshops, the MindFest, or both. The observations have been used to craft a more detailed evaluation plan, which was submitted for review to the PIE staff in the fall of 2001. The evaluation plan is included below and is based on these observations as well as CCT participation in the staff debriefing and on comments posted by PIE staffers.

CCT conducted interviews with various Fort Worth staff members 5 months after the workshops and the MindFest to augment their observations and interviews conducted during and immediately after the events.This work will be available in a PIE interim evaluation report to be completed in March 2002. This work represents the initial efforts to address questions of PIE�s impact on project staff and participating institutions, which is one of the main strands of CCT�s evaluation inquiry.

CCT is also developing a tool to gauge impact on participants in PIE workshops and events, which is an equally important strand of CCT�s inquiry.One conclusion coming out of the Fort Worth experience and that of other PIE-related workshops conducted at partners� institutions is that developing projects using crickets and other materials that are not possible to take home (due to cost and availability constraints) creates some frustration for both young and old inventors.CCT is working on a data collection technique that will offer parents and guardians a digital photographic record of their invention or their child�s invention. At the same time, CCT researchers and museum staff will use a structured interview with the inventor(s) to get a description and underlying vision of the invented artifact. CCT is crafting questions as part of this interview to get at experiences, skills and concepts relevant to larger technology literacy constructs that may have been fostered by the play/design experience that the inventor went through in building and revising her/his invention. These constructs are being derived from the various science and technology standards documents with the goal of being able to show evidence of how participation in PIE experiences relates to the development of key technology concepts and skills.

This research process will also examine how to fit research into the event context without being disruptive to that experience.It will also address a new model for obtaining informed consent from parents and guardians.

CCT will be sharing drafts of this process with the PIE Staff and will begin pilot testing of the procedure at the MIT Museum at the end of April with subsequent refinement during events held in the summer and fall of 2002.

3. Training

Staff Workshop
During the week prior to the first MindFest at the Fort Worth Museum of Science and History (August 21-25, 2001), staff from all of the PIE Network museums gathered in Fort Worth for an intensive week-long staff-development workshop. The staff workshop involved PIE staff in:

  • sharing and discussing examples of activities that integrate the digital and physical worlds;
  • learning about new MIT cricket hardware and software developments; and
  • working on new cricket-controlled playful inventions and explorations.

After the week-long workshop, staff from all of the museums helped set up equipment, organize activities, and run workshops at the Fort Worth MindFest, held on Saturday, August 26. The day after the MindFest, PIE Network staff gathered with evaluation staff from Center for Children and Technology (CCT) to share observations and discuss each aspect of the event. Notes from the meeting (by PIE staff at three museums and by CCT staff) were compiled and posted on the PIE staff website, and are helping inform planning for future MindFest events atother PIE museums.

Museum-to-Museum and MIT-to-Museum Staff Development
Throughout the first year of the project, MIT Media Lab and other PIE project staff have provided a network of support to project staff from individual museums. The primary format for providing support has been participatory facilitation -- that is, helping staff learn by collaborating with them in leading workshops for visitors. For example:

  • In preparation for the Fort Worth MindFest, Chip Lindsey from the Fort Worth Museum came to the Exploratorium to observe and learn during a Marble Machines and Pinball workshop using cricket technology. Mike Petrich from the Exploratorium then went to Fort Worth to assist with a similar Marble Machines workshop led by Chip Lindsey and Julie Cross-Steele in Fort Worth.
  • In July 2001, Diane Willow and Casey Smith (of the MIT Media Lab) and Meg Goetz (a PIE consultant) introduced American Visionary Art Museum (AVAM) staff to crickets in preparation for the weeklong staff development workshop.
  • In July 2001, Meg Goetz and Morgan L'Argent from the Science Museum of Minnesota (SMM) led another staff development workshop for new PIE staff at Lemelson and from AVAM.
  • In November 2001, Meg Goetz and Rahul Bhargava of the MIT Media Lab worked with Anita Cater at the Lemelson Center to set up and run an open house for staff from the National Museum of American History, in preparation for their MindFest event, to be held in August 2002.
  • Anita Cater, Meg Goetz, and Rahul Bhargava helped at the Exquisite Trash event at AVAM in November 2001.
  • Diane Willow and several graduates students from the MIT Media Lab have met regularly and collaborated with Stephanie Hunt at the MIT Museum on the development and implementation of their ongoing series of Invention Studio workshops.

PIE Staff Introducing Cricket Technology to Other Staff at Their Museums
PIE staff at several museums sites also took the initiative to provide other staff at their museums hands-on experience with cricket technology. For example, Fort Worth PIE staff, in preparation for their MindFest in August 2001, set up a space forother museum staff to try out cricket motors, sensors, and LogoBlocks programming. PIE staff from the Science Museum of Minnesota held an open house to introduce cricket technology to others on the museum staff.And PIE staff at the Lemelson Center held an introductory session for 20 staff members from the National Museum of American History to learn more about the "Invention at Play" exhibition.

PIE Staff Web Site
Science Museum of Minnesota staff developed a web site for all PIE Network project staff. The main purpose of the site is to make accessible in one place the growing experience and findings across the network. The staff site includes:

  • the latest PIE Network news
  • a calendar of PIE events (and relevant events outside the PIE Network)
  • a scrapbook linking toreflections, reports, photos, and other media on PIE workshops and events
  • a place to share ideas and brainstorming for future MIT cricket technology development
  • other related resources

The PIE staff web site is also designed to serve as a resource for future PIE initiatives, including developing PIE Pamphlets, writing the MindFest Manual, and training staff from other museums as part of dissemination of the PIE project.
